# Graph Isomorphism from First Principles: Symmetry, Algebra, and Algorithms

## 1. Graph Isomorphism from First Principles

Welcome. This talk explains two papers on graph isomorphism, starting from zero. No background is assumed beyond knowing what a dot and a line are. We will build up the ideas step by step: what it means for two graphs to be the same, what a symmetry is, how the standard tools work and where they break, and then what the two papers add. At the end we run the software together on real examples, and every number on the slides can be reproduced from the public repository.

## 2. Seven steps, from dots to the bound

The plan. Part 1: what a graph is, what it means for two graphs to be the same, and the language of symmetry: groups, generators, orbits. Part 2: colour refinement, the workhorse of every practical solver, and the graphs built to defeat it. Part 3 is the first paper, Refinement-derived algebraic computation of graph automorphism groups: refinement proposes candidate maps, linear algebra decides. Part 4 is the second paper, A practical graph isomorphism solver and an explicit quasi-polynomial bound: symmetry that is not abelian, invariant cuts and Luks' method. Part 5 is that paper's theory: Babai's giants, the bound in bits and conjecture G2. Then the evidence, live runs, and a glossary of every term defined along the way.

## 3. The contribution in one sentence

Before the details, here is the whole contribution in one sentence. We use invariant refinement to expose algebraic structure, solve the resulting symmetry constraints, and combine that practical solver with a fully specified quasi-polynomial fallback and explicit branching-bit accounting. One distinction runs through the talk. The executable is what the public code runs: a colouring, the series solver, the stabiliser chain, and the cut and star routes with Luks' method. It is exact: when it answers, the answer is right. The theoretical hybrid is the same pipeline with Babai's giant case added as the last fallback, and the worst-case bound counts every stage, including that one. The giant case is specified line by line but not implemented, because no instance ever reached it. Finally, two conjectures come out of this work, and they may be its most valuable part: Conjecture 11.1, that candidate sets are always groups, and Conjecture G2, that the giant case is never needed. Both are stated precisely, and we come back to them at the end of part 5.

## 4. Graphs and isomorphism

Two definitions to start. A graph is a set of vertices and a set of edges, where each edge is an unordered pair of vertices; n denotes the number of vertices. An isomorphism from G to H is a renaming of the vertices, a bijection, that maps edges to edges and non-edges to non-edges. The two drawings show one and the same graph, because the edge list is identical: ab, bc, cd, da. The drawing plays no role. The graph isomorphism problem, GI, asks: given two graphs, does an isomorphism exist?

## 5. Why it is hard: too many renamings

Why is this hard? The naive approach is to try every renaming. With 4 vertices there are 24, with 10 there are already over three and a half million, and with 50 vertices the number has 65 digits. So brute force is out of the question even for small graphs. Every useful algorithm exploits structure: degrees, neighbourhoods, symmetry. The complexity status is famous: nobody knows a polynomial-time algorithm, and GI is not known to be NP-complete; a proof that it is would have major consequences in complexity theory, namely the collapse of the polynomial hierarchy to its second level. In 2015 Babai announced a quasi-polynomial algorithm, a huge theoretical breakthrough that we come back to in part 5. Note on the slide: 50 factorial is about 3 times 10 to the 64; the Earth has roughly 10 to the 50 atoms.

## 6. A symmetry is an automorphism

An automorphism is an isomorphism from a graph to itself: a renaming of the vertices under which every edge becomes an edge again. Aut of G is the set of all automorphisms. Any renaming is a permutation of the vertices; we write it by listing images, a goes to b, b to c and so on. Check the quarter turn: edge ab becomes bc, bc becomes cd, cd becomes da, da becomes ab. Every edge lands on an edge, so it is an automorphism. The square has eight: the turns by 0, 90, 180 and 270 degrees, and four mirrors, two along the diagonals and two through the middles of opposite sides; two mirror lines are drawn dashed.

## 7. Symmetries form a group

Symmetries can be composed: do one, then the other, and you get a symmetry again. The identity, do nothing, is a symmetry, and every symmetry can be undone. A set of permutations with these properties is called a permutation group, and its number of elements is its order. A set S generates the group if every element is obtained by composing elements of S, and then S is called a list of generators. This is how every solver stores the answer: the benchmark graph CHH(7-7)-2 has about 7 times 10 to the 70 automorphisms, far too many to list, but a short list of generators determines all of them, and from it the order can be computed exactly, by the Schreier-Sims algorithm. The next slide shows generators at work on the square.

## 8. Two generators give all 8 symmetries

Here are generators at work. Take r, the quarter turn, and s, the mirror on the diagonal through a and c, which swaps b and d. Compose them, with the convention that rs means first s, then r. The table lists every product r to the i, or r to the i times s, for i from 0 to 3, with the image of each vertex. Each row is checked: all eight map every edge to an edge, and they are all different. Four turns, four mirrors. So two generators describe the whole group of order 8. For a group of order 10 to the 70 the same principle holds: a handful of generators, and algorithms like Schreier-Sims compute the order from them without listing anything. For the advanced viewer: this is the dihedral group of order 8.

## 9. Orbits and stabilisers

Two more definitions, used throughout. The orbit of a vertex v is the set of all vertices a symmetry can move v to. The stabiliser of v is the set of symmetries that leave v where it is; it is a group itself. The orbit-stabiliser theorem connects them: the order of the group is the size of the orbit times the order of the stabiliser. On the square: a can be sent to every vertex by the turns, so its orbit has 4 vertices; reading the table, only the identity and the mirror s keep a fixed, so the stabiliser has 2 elements, and 4 times 2 is 8, as it should be. Repeating this gives a stabiliser chain: fix a, then look at the orbit of b inside the stabiliser of a, which is b and d; fix b too and only the identity remains. So the order is 4 times 2 times 1. This is exactly how the stabiliser chain in part 3 computes group orders.

## 10. One computation answers both questions

This is why it suffices to compute automorphism groups. The disjoint union G plus H is the two graphs side by side. If G and H are connected, every automorphism of the union either keeps each side in place or swaps the two sides, and a swap exists exactly when G and H are isomorphic; restricted to G, a swapping automorphism is an isomorphism from G to H. If a graph is disconnected one works with complements, which preserves isomorphism. Even counting decides it: if isomorphic, the union's group is twice the product, because of the swap; otherwise it is just the product. The table shows a real test: an S3 CFI graph with 1,296 automorphisms against a shuffled copy gives 3,359,232, which is 2 times 1,296 squared, so isomorphic; against its twisted version, which has 432 automorphisms, it gives 559,872, the plain product, so not isomorphic. S3-CFI graphs are explained in part 4; you run exactly this at the end.

## 11. Colour refinement: let the neighbours vote

Two definitions. A colouring assigns a colour to each vertex, and a class is the set of vertices sharing a colour. Colour refinement repeats one round: each vertex's new colour records its old colour together with the multiset, meaning the list with repetitions, of its neighbours' colours. The colouring is stable when a round changes nothing. On the path of five vertices: at the start everyone is alike. After round 1 the ends, with one neighbour, separate from the inner vertices, with two. After round 2 the vertices next to an end, which see one end-coloured neighbour, separate from the middle vertex, which sees none. Round 3 changes nothing: three classes, stable. The algorithm is also called 1-WL, the one-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm, and runs in time O of n plus m times log n.

## 12. Refinement never lies, but may not tell all

A colouring is invariant when it is computed from the structure only, never from the names of the vertices. Colour refinement is invariant. Consequence: every automorphism maps each vertex to a vertex of the same colour, so each class is a union of orbits. If refinement makes every class a single vertex, the only automorphism is the identity and we are done. But the converse fails: two vertices in one class need not lie in the same orbit. A class can suggest much more symmetry than the graph really has. The whole difficulty of graph isomorphism lives in this gap, and it reappears in part 5 under the name loose giant.

## 13. Where refinement is blind

Here is refinement's blind spot. On the left, K3,3: three vertices on top, each joined to all three at the bottom. On the right, the triangular prism: two triangles joined by three spokes. In both graphs every vertex has exactly three neighbours, so after round 1 nothing can split: every vertex keeps the same colour forever. Refinement sees the two graphs as identical. But they are not isomorphic: the prism contains triangles and K3,3 contains none. Graphs where every vertex looks alike to refinement, called regular graphs, are where solvers have to work harder.

## 14. Individualise and refine

When refinement gets stuck, solvers individualise: give one vertex a colour of its own and refine again, which usually splits a lot more. The sequence of individualised vertices is called a base, and a leaf is reached when every class is a single vertex. Since we do not know where a symmetry would send our chosen vertex, we branch over all vertices it could be mapped to, which gives a search tree. Two leaves with the same colour pattern define a renaming: match up the vertices with the same colour, and test whether the renaming is an automorphism. Automorphisms found so far let the search skip branches that can only repeat known results, shown in grey. This is the individualisation-refinement paradigm behind nauty, Traces, bliss and saucy. Traces is the state of the art, and in our work it is used only as an independent oracle to check answers.

## 15. Graphs built to defeat the search

Researchers have built graphs on purpose to defeat refinement. The classic is the CFI construction of Cai, Fürer and Immerman, 1992. Start from a base graph; replace each vertex by a small gadget and each edge by a pair of wires; a twist crosses one pair. The twisted and untwisted graphs are not isomorphic, but every gadget looks identical to refinement, so the twist cannot be located locally: it could be anywhere. Neuen and Schweitzer pushed this to multipedes: rigid graphs, meaning their only automorphism is the identity, on which every individualisation-refinement algorithm needs exponentially many leaves. Our benchmark contains several such families: cfi-rigid, which are multipedes, sat_cfi_dim, chh and tnn. They are the motivating test of both papers.

## 16. Refinement proposes, algebra decides

Part 3 covers the first paper, Refinement-derived algebraic computation of graph automorphism groups. Its idea in one line: refinement proposes, class by class, a set of candidate maps, and algebra decides which combinations are real symmetries, by solving equations instead of searching. We first define precisely what a candidate is, with a worked example.

## 17. What is a candidate? Worked on the square

Here is the central notion of the first paper, worked out completely. Fix a class C of the colouring, and a base: a sequence of vertices of C that, once individualised, makes every vertex of C a singleton. On the square, all four vertices are one class. Take base a, b. Individualising a splits the class into a, its two neighbours b and d, and the opposite vertex c; individualising b then separates d. Now try images: a tuple s is admissible if individualising it, step by step, produces the same pattern of class sizes as the base did. x can be any of the four vertices; y must then be one of the two neighbours of x, because that is where b sat. Each admissible s gives a candidate: map every vertex to the vertex that ends with the same colour. For s equal to c, d we get a to c, b to d, c to a, d to b, the half turn. Altogether 4 times 2 is 8 candidates, and on the square they are exactly the 8 symmetries. On harder graphs they are not; see the next slide.

## 18. The candidate set T_C: never misses

The set of all candidates of a class C is called T_C. The restriction of an automorphism to C means simply what it does on the vertices of C. The guarantee is one-sided: every automorphism, restricted to C, appears among the candidates, so T_C never misses a real symmetry. But a candidate can be fake: consistent on C, yet impossible on the whole graph. On CFI graphs this happens on a large scale: the candidate set of a class can have 2 to the k elements while the graph has only the identity as automorphism; the equations of the next slide remove the fakes. Whether T_C is always a group is Conjecture 11.1 of the first paper; part 4 proves it when admissibility is checked on all vertices, and we return to it near the end. For the advanced viewer: T_C equals the set of tau_s over admissible s and contains the restriction of Aut(G); it equals it when the leaves are discrete on all vertices and admissibility is checked on all vertices.

## 19. Edges between classes become equations

This slide is the heart of the first paper. A group is abelian when the order of composition does not matter. Modulo 2 arithmetic means 1 plus 1 is 0. On a CFI graph each gadget gets a bit, 1 for flipped, and the candidate groups consist of bit flips, which are abelian. The wires allow only some combinations of flips, and those rules are linear equations modulo 2. In the toy example the third equation is the sum of the first two, so the rank is 2; with three unknowns there are 2 to the 3 minus 2, that is 2, solutions: flip nothing, or flip all three. Gaussian elimination finds and counts the solutions in polynomial time, where a search would try 2 to the 1,000 combinations for a thousand gadgets. For the advanced viewer, one distinction matters. T_C is only a set of maps, and we have just said it need not be a group; the angle brackets around T_C denote the group it generates, which may be larger. The solver works with groups, so it computes a generating set and checks at run time that the generated group stays inside T_C, that is, T_C equals the group it generates. In every run it did; correctness never depends on this, because it is checked. The paper then takes a normal series of T_C whose factors are elementary abelian, vectors over a prime field F_p, and asks that each allowed set be a coset, which is also checked. Each layer turns the edges between classes into affine systems over F_p, and solutions are counted coset by coset. It is the classical tower method for bounded colour multiplicity, applied without assuming bounded multiplicity.

## 20. When T_C is too big: a stabiliser chain

Sometimes the candidate groups are too big, typically when the local symmetry is large. Then the first paper uses a stabiliser chain, the classical tool we saw on the square. Fix a root, find its orbit, fix it, move to the next root, and multiply the orbit sizes. The new part is how an orbit is decided: to know whether some symmetry sends r to x, individualise r in one copy of the graph and x in the other, and let the algebraic solver compare the two. The answer is either an explicit automorphism, verified edge by edge, or a proof that none exists. Which roots to pick? They come from a new decomposition called dialysis, on the next slide. On the square, roots a and b give orbit sizes 4, 2 and 1, so 8 again.

## 21. T_C and the chain on Johnson graphs

Now a real run of the first paper's machinery, copied from the logs of the benchmark campaign. A Johnson graph J of n and k has one vertex for every k-element subset of 1 to n, and two subsets are adjacent when they share all but one element. Every vertex looks the same to refinement, so there is a single class. On J of 6 and 3, with 20 vertices, the candidate set T C of that class has 1,440 elements, it is a group, and every candidate is a real symmetry: the 720 permutations of the six points, times complementation. The algebraic solver alone answers in 0.28 seconds. On J of 8 and 3, with 56 vertices, the candidate set is too large: more than 20,000 candidates, so the solver stops at depth 0 and the stabiliser chain takes over. It fixes one vertex r, a 3-element set. Refinement now splits the other vertices by how many elements they share with r: 3, 2, 1 or 0, giving classes of 1, 15, 30 and 10 vertices. On this coloured graph the candidate groups and the equations work, and give the stabiliser of r, with 720 elements: 3 factorial times 5 factorial, permuting the three points inside r and the five outside. Then the chain asks for the orbit of r: all 56 vertices are reached, with one two-sided query. So the order is 56 times 720, which is 40,320, exactly 8 factorial, the known answer. The whole run takes 1.2 seconds on the Xeon.

## 22. Dialysis and the colouring Π

Dialysis is the rooted decomposition introduced in the first paper. From a root, go out layer by layer by distance. A vertex joins the tree if it has exactly one neighbour in the tree in the previous layer, its parent. An orphan is a vertex outside the tree all of whose neighbours lie in the tree. What remains splits into connected components, the remainders, and each remainder is peeled again from its own root. The picture is a schematic: the orange orphan hangs on two tree vertices; the grey remainder has two parents and further neighbours outside the tree. Dialysis supplies the roots of the stabiliser chain, and it also gives a colouring called Pi: start from refinement, split classes by the canonical shape of each vertex's dialysis tree together with its number of orphans, then by the colourings of its remainders, refining after each split. Pi is invariant, at least as fine as plain refinement, and costs O of v times v plus e times log v. Intuitively, Pi sees the shape of the graph around each vertex; it plays a key role in part 5.

## 23. What the first paper achieves

The first paper in numbers. All 12,001 instances of the 36 benchmark families are solved exactly, 10,911 by the algebraic solver alone. Precisely what that means: the solver does individualise, once per candidate, to build each T_C, but it never backtracks over choices; everything after the candidates is linear algebra. The other 1,090 needed the stabiliser chain, which does search. Traces, run separately as an oracle, agrees wherever it answered. Proofs: the solver is polynomial on the multipedes, where every individualisation-refinement algorithm is exponential. And all Johnson and Hamming graphs are shown to be Tinhofer graphs, a notion of Arvind, Köbler, Rattan and Verbitsky: whatever tuple of vertices you individualise, refinement then finds exactly the orbits, so the stabiliser chain never has to search, and it is polynomial. Johnson graphs have k-element subsets as vertices, adjacent when they share k minus 1 elements; Hamming graphs have words as vertices, adjacent when they differ in one letter. On projective planes the Tinhofer property fails, and the chain attains the same N to the O of log log N bound as Miller's classical argument.

## 24. When the hidden symmetry is not abelian

Parts 4 and 5 cover the second paper, A practical graph isomorphism solver and an explicit quasi-polynomial bound. Its starting point: the linear algebra of the first paper needs abelian local symmetry, where the order of operations does not matter. Some graphs hide symmetry where order does matter. Part 4 shows how the practical solver handles them; part 5 asks how bad the worst case can be, with explicit numbers.

## 25. Group-CFI: gadgets where order matters

The test family of the second paper is group-CFI. Take the CFI construction, but let wires carry elements of a group Gamma instead of bits, and let a gadget accept a triple g1, g2, g3 exactly when the product g1 g2 g3, in this order, is the identity. With Gamma equal to S3, the six symmetries of a triangle, or A5, the sixty rotations of an icosahedron, the order of multiplication matters, so the rules are not linear equations, and the first paper's algebra stalls. S3 is the smallest non-abelian group; A5 is not even solvable, meaning it cannot be built from abelian layers at all. Traces struggles too: on S3 group-CFI over a random cubic base, it needs 483 seconds at 1,080 vertices, is killed without an answer after 6,124 seconds on the twisted graph, and at 1,296 vertices after 45 seconds; larger sizes were not run. Our solver answers in seconds up to 3,456 vertices.

## 26. Cut along an invariant edge class

The first new tool of the second paper is the invariant cut. An edge class is the set of all edges whose two ends carry a given pair of colours. Since the colouring is invariant, every symmetry maps each edge class to itself, so deleting a class loses no symmetry information: that is an invariant cut. What remains falls apart into pieces; pieces that are isomorphic share a type; and the quotient is a small graph with one node per piece, recording which pieces the cut edges joined. In the picture, refinement colours the three apices orange, because they are the only vertices with a wire, so all wire edges join orange to orange and form one class. Cutting them leaves three triangles of one type, and the quotient is a triangle of three nodes.

## 27. The (D) route: pieces, then Luks

The decomposition route, called the (D) route, works in three steps. First, compute the automorphism group of each piece, recursively, with the same algorithm. Second, form the group P of all combinations: a symmetry inside each piece, together with the permutations of pieces of one type that the quotient allows. Every automorphism of G lies in P, but P is usually much bigger. Third, Luks' method, a classical algorithm from 1982, takes a group and a structure, here the set of cut edges, and returns exactly the members of the group that preserve the structure. It works recursively over block systems, and it is fast as long as the groups it meets along the way have small building blocks. The table shows real runs: S3 group-CFI on 648 vertices, 12 pieces, quotient group of order 4, 36 automorphisms in under a second; the twisted version, 12. A5 group-CFI on the complete graph K4: 15,120 vertices, 4 pieces, quotient group of order 24, 57,600 automorphisms in 77 seconds. The last column, the largest giant met, is explained in part 5: for now, note it is small.

## 28. Luks on an S₃-CFI graph, step by step

And a real run of Luks' method, from the second paper's logs, on the S3 group-CFI graph with 648 vertices that you will also run yourself at the end. The invariant cut removes 108 edges and leaves 12 pieces, all of one isomorphism type. Each piece has 1,296 symmetries, computed from 7 generators. The group P lets every piece move by any of its own symmetries independently, so it has 1,296 to the 12, about 2 times 10 to the 37 elements, acting on the 216 endpoints of the cut edges. Luks' method must find the members of P that map cut edges to cut edges. It never lists P; it shrinks it. A refinement step first splits the endpoints from 12 colours to 36, and the group drops to about 10 to the 28. Then thirteen orbit steps divide the group by 36 each time, down to about 60 million. Another refinement with four orbit steps brings it to 1,296, and a last refinement with two orbit steps to 36. Finally the quotient: Q, the symmetries of the way the pieces are joined, has order 4. Four isomorphism tests show that no non-trivial element of Q lifts to a symmetry of the graph. So the automorphism group has order 36, which is what Traces reports; for the twisted graph the same steps end at 12. No giant appears anywhere, and the whole run takes 0.89 seconds on the Xeon.

## 29. The whole solver: each stage is a fallback

This is the whole practical solver, as a chain of fallbacks. A colouring first: plain refinement or Pi. Then the series solver of the first paper: candidate groups and equations. If it stops, the stabiliser chain with dialysis roots. If that stops, typically because of non-abelian local symmetry, the decomposition route: invariant cut and Luks. If no edge class disconnects the graph, the star route: for each class C it builds the star of C, the subgraph on C plus, for every other class, one node for each distinct set of C-neighbours that its vertices have; the automorphisms of that structure acting on C form the star group, which contains every symmetry restricted to C, and Luks works over the product of these groups. When exactly does Luks come in? Only when the stabiliser chain stops, on its time limit, its work limit or its candidate cap. Not abelian versus non-abelian as such: the series solver handles some non-abelian groups itself, for example the Johnson graph J of 6 and 3. But in practice the chain stops when the local symmetry is non-abelian, as on group-CFI graphs over S3 or A5, because the rules on the edges between classes are then no longer linear equations. Every stage is exact. The final stage is Babai's giant case: specified line by line and included in the worst-case bound, but deliberately not implemented, because no instance ever reached it, and untested code is a risk in an exact solver.

## 30. “Why not cut into pieces, run the pipeline on each, and skip Luks?”

A natural question at this point: why not cut the graph into pieces, run the existing pipeline on each piece, and avoid Luks altogether? The answer is that we already do run the pipeline on each piece. A piece is solved by the series solver, then by the cut route on the piece, whose own pieces recurse, then by the star route. Even the pattern of how pieces are joined is turned into a graph and solved by the series solver; that gives the quotient group Q. What the pipeline cannot do is the gluing. Knowing the symmetries of every piece is not enough. In the S3 CFI example each of the 12 pieces has 1,296 symmetries, so combining them freely gives 1,296 to the 12, about 2 times 10 to the 37 combinations, and only 36 of them keep the 108 cut edges intact. The question is: which members of a group we already know, far too large to list, preserve a structure? The pipeline answers questions about graphs; this is a question about a given group. That is exactly Luks' method: it never lists the group, it shrinks it, from 10 to the 37 down to 36 in under a second. Stars and balls are the same story. They are ambient groups, groups known to contain every symmetry restricted to a class; the pipeline computes them, because a star group is the symmetry group of a smaller structure, but their combination still overcounts, and cutting it down to the true group is again Luks' job. And rerunning the pipeline on the whole graph is exactly what had stopped. For the advanced viewer: the gluing step is a special case of multiple-coset isomorphism, and the paper remarks that Wiebking's theorem suggests a version of the cut route that is quasi-polynomial in the largest piece rather than in n, a condition we have not verified for our cuts.

## 31. When the candidates form a group

One theoretical result of the second paper feeds straight back into the solver. Recall the open question: are the candidates of a class always a group? Change one detail of the test: at every step, compare the class sizes of all vertices, not just those inside C. We call this admissibility on V. A fractional automorphism is a soft symmetry: a matrix of non-negative numbers whose rows and columns each sum to one, which commutes with the adjacency matrix, and which only links vertices of the same colour. A permutation matrix with AX equal to XA is an ordinary automorphism. Tinhofer, and Ramana, Scheinerman and Ullman, showed that these matrices are exactly what colour refinement can see. The proposition, a short consequence of their results that we could not find stated, says: with admissibility on V, the candidates of C are exactly the permutation blocks of fractional automorphisms. Since products of fractional automorphisms are fractional automorphisms, the candidates form a group. The payoff: the solver prunes its search with this group, and that is now proved safe without any conjecture. Conditional answers became exact, and on S3 group-CFI on rc12 the stage finishes in 7 seconds instead of running out its 1,800-second budget. Where both versions finish, the pruning costs a median factor of 1.17.

## 32. How fast can GI be solved in the worst case?

Part 5 is about the worst case. A quasi-polynomial running time is exponential in a power of log n. With power 1 that is just polynomial, and every fixed power still grows far slower than exponential in any root of n. The history: in 1983 Babai and Luks reached exponential in the square root of n log n, the record for over thirty years. In 2015 Babai announced a quasi-polynomial algorithm; Helfgott showed the power can be 3; Dona made the constants explicit in 2021. Babai's algorithm actually solves String Isomorphism: given a permutation group acting on the positions of a string, and two strings, decide whether some group element turns one string into the other. Graph isomorphism reduces to it, with the positions being pairs of vertices.

## 33. The one obstacle: giants

Now the obstacle, defined precisely. Sym of m is the group of all permutations of m points, m factorial of them; Alt of m is the subgroup of even permutations, half as many. Luks' method processes a group through block systems; at a step where the group acts on m blocks, with m at least 5, as at least Alt of m, we say it meets a giant of degree m. A giant is genuine if the true symmetries of the string, in our case of the graph, already act on those blocks as at least Alt of m; otherwise it is loose, symmetry that the current group suggests but the graph does not have. Why is it the obstacle? Luks' method branches over the blocks, and a giant can mean m factorial choices. Genuine giants occur: in Johnson graphs, whose vertices are k-subsets of m points, the symmetry really is the full symmetric group on the points. Babai's breakthrough, local certificates, decides in quasi-polynomial time whether a giant is genuine and how to proceed if it is not. That is the giant case.

## 34. Counting the cost in bits

To compare algorithms in the worst case, we count bits: the base-2 logarithm of the number of branches. Ten bits means about a thousand branches, twenty bits about a million. The second paper makes the constants in front explicit. For String Isomorphism, the general problem Babai's algorithm actually solves, the cost is at most 11.4 times log N cubed bits, a factor of about 3.7 smaller than the bound of the published explicit analysis by Dona, for the same algorithm, converted to the same units: his 6 times (1 plus 1 over ln 2) cubed times ln n cubed is 42.0 log2 N cubed; the figure he states, 100 ln cubed n, is 48.0. For graphs on v vertices it is 43.4 times log v cubed, for the hybrid algorithm that uses Babai's giant case as its last fallback. And if a hypothesis called D_G holds, the giant case is never needed and the constant drops to 6.84. To keep perspective: for a thousand vertices that is about 43,000 bits, a number of branches with roughly 13,000 digits. This is a guarantee for the worst imaginable graph, not what happens in practice; in practice our runs take seconds. The exponent 3 is Helfgott's and we do not improve it.

## 35. Conjecture G2: the giant never really comes

The second paper asks: is the giant case ever needed in this framework? It states a precise hypothesis, called D_G, that is checked branch by branch. On each branch, either the chain finishes within a small budget, as it does on projective planes, or every giant that Luks' method meets is genuine, meaning real symmetry, or is small, of degree at most m zero, where brute force is affordable within the bound. m zero is the maximum of 91 and 10 times the floor of log2 N plus 31, where N, at most v squared, is the string length; so it is at least 91. Conjecture G2 says every graph satisfies D_G when we start from the structural colouring Pi. If true, the bound drops from 43.4 to 6.84, and Babai's giant case is not needed for this algorithm. The evidence: the giant case was never reached on the 12,001 benchmark instances, nor in five rounds of constructions designed specifically to break G2, and the largest giant ever met with Pi had degree 26, far below 91. This is evidence, not proof, and the paper says so.

## 36. A loose giant is a question to the invariant first

Here is a lesson from the experiments. A loose giant is symmetry that the current invariant sees but the graph does not have: exactly the gap between looks-symmetric and is-symmetric from part 2. On a chh-type graph with 1,408 vertices, plain refinement leaves a class on which Luks would meet the full symmetric group on 168 points, while the true symmetry there is far smaller, about 10 to the 93 elements against the 168 factorial over 2 a genuine giant would need. Switch to the colouring Pi, and its classes are exactly the orbits of the automorphism group: the giant disappears, and the graph is solved in 13 seconds. So before concluding that Babai's machinery is needed, ask first whether a better invariant dissolves the giant. Honest caveat on the slide: 168 is below this graph's threshold of 231, so it is not a counterexample to anything; it shows the mechanism.

## 37. An open challenge: break G2

The paper ends its theory part with an open challenge, and states exactly what a counterexample has to show. First, a graph where, starting from Pi and with the recursion inside pieces, the chain does not finish within its budget. Second, a loose giant above the threshold m zero that appears for every allowed choice of ambient groups, stars or balls. Such a graph refutes G2 as stated. But it would not by itself show that Babai's group-theoretic machinery is needed: for that, the loose giant would have to survive every polynomial-time invariant one could add, a much stronger demand. Conversely, if a stronger invariant dissolves a loose giant, that suggests a refinement of the algorithm, but does not prove G2. If you enjoy constructing hard graphs, this is an invitation.

## 38. Perhaps the most valuable part

Two conjectures come out of this work, and they may be worth more than any single result in the papers. The first is Conjecture 11.1 of the first paper. If a base discretises a class C, then the candidate set T_C is a group, and in fact it equals the symmetry, restricted to C, of some graph G prime that refinement cannot tell apart from G locally. What is known: no counterexample appeared in any run; on multipedes it is a theorem, for every round limit; and the second paper proves the group part when admissibility is checked on all vertices, via fractional automorphisms. What is open: admissibility checked on C only, which can give a strictly larger set, for example 8 candidates against 4 symmetries on two graphs with 8 vertices, still a group there, and round limits below stability. Why it matters: refinement alone would then produce groups, not merely sets that contain the symmetry. The second is Conjecture G2 of the second paper: every graph satisfies the dichotomy D_G when we start from Pi, so no branch ever needs Babai's giant case. The evidence is the 12,001 instances, five rounds of constructions aimed at it, and a largest giant of degree 26. A proof would bring the bound from 43.4 down to 6.84 times log v cubed bits with no giant case; a counterexample would be just as interesting, as the previous slide explained.

## 39. Against Traces: honest in both directions

How does this compare with the state of the art? We ran Traces on the same machine as our solver. The honest summary goes both ways. Traces is faster on 27 of the 36 benchmark families, often by a large factor; it is a superbly engineered tool. But on the families built to defeat search, the multipedes and the CFI-type families, Traces stops answering from some size on: 178 benchmark instances got no answer, and our solver answered all of them exactly. Even below that size, where Traces is faster in the median, its worst case is much worse: on cfi-rigid-t2 its slowest answer took 3,407 seconds while ours never took more than a quarter of a second on the same instances. The recommendation of the paper: use Traces in general, and our solver on these families and on the group-CFI diagnostics, above the sizes stated per family.

## 40. The hardest family: random class-2 groups

The hardest family we know for our solver is the multiplication tables of random groups of class 2, turned into graphs: the Latin square graph of a group of order |G| has |G| squared vertices. These groups have very few automorphisms, and finding them is like finding a needle. Here Traces is clearly better: at order 128 it is 4 to 14 times faster than our solver. All our times are in look mode, which adds two sound options: pruning by stabiliser orbits and a lookahead. Without them, in base mode, the order-243 run was stopped by hand after 10,910 seconds, and the paper reports that too. With look mode the solver stays exact as the groups grow: order 243 with 59,049 vertices in 1,000 seconds and order 256 with 65,536 vertices in 6,468 seconds. The group orders were checked against an independent program: for these graphs the automorphism group has order |G| squared times 6 times the order of Aut G, and Aut G was computed separately by backtracking.

## 41. Where the cost really comes from

A word on what happens in practice, as opposed to the worst-case bound. In every run, the terms that make the bound quasi-polynomial stayed inactive. Babai's giant case was never needed. The largest giant met with the colouring Pi had degree 26, while the threshold m zero is at least 91, and on the group-CFI graphs no giant had degree above 8. On the families where we measured growth, the cost grew like n to a power b between about 0.9 and 2.8. So in practice the cost comes from refinement, from the algebra and from the refutation search of the stabiliser chain. What is proved on the hard combinatorial families are quasi-polynomial bounds: n to the O of log n for Latin squares and Steiner triple systems, which match Miller's classical bounds, and for Hadamard graphs, there by individualisation-refinement along a computed base; and N to the O of log log N on projective planes. The measured growth is far lower on Latin squares and Steiner triple systems, with exponents of about 1.4 and 1.6, while on the planes our measurements are budget-capped and say little. These are observations about the instances we ran, not bounds.

## 42. Build the solver and make a graph

Now let us run it. You need Linux, a Java development kit version 17 or newer, a C compiler, because three small native libraries are built from source, and Python 3. Clone the public repository, and build the jar with the wrapper script, which runs the build inside a memory-capped scope so it cannot freeze a desktop. Then make a work folder outside the repository and generate two small instances: the S3 group-CFI graph on a random cubic graph with 12 vertices, seed 1, once untwisted and once twisted. Each has 648 vertices and 1,404 edges, in the simple DIMACS text format: a header line, then one line per edge.

## 43. One graph through the whole flow

Example 1 sends one graph through the full flow of the paper. The settings in capitals switch on the pieces we discussed: TCPRUNE with ADMIT_V is the leaf-group pruning, made safe by the proposition; TCGROUP, AUTPRUNE and GENAUT are the group-building options of the series solver; LUKS and LUKS_PLAIN hand over to Luks' method if the series stage stops; ONE_USE_PI starts from the colouring Pi; ONE_DEADLINE_S is the series stage's budget in seconds. The script safe_java_test.sh runs one Java process with a 1,000 MB heap inside a memory-capped scope; its arguments are a unit name, the heap, the class, the method and the log file. The output shown is a real run on a laptop: status EXACT, 36 automorphisms, in 6.4 seconds, with a peak of 333 MB. EXACT means proved; COND_EXACT would mean proved if a conjecture holds; a STOP reason means no answer within the limits, never a wrong one. Exit code 3 means not enough free memory to start: put FLOOR_MB=500 in front of the command on a busy desktop. The twisted graph gives 12.

## 44. An isomorphism test you can run

Example 2 is the isomorphism test from part 1, for real. Generate the S3 group-CFI graph on a random cubic graph with 16 vertices, untwisted and twisted. The script disjoint_union.py puts two graphs side by side in one file; the last argument 7 shuffles the second copy by a random permutation, so the first pair is isomorphic but not identical. The second pair joins the graph with its twisted version. Then the decomposition route computes the automorphism group of both unions. The numbers are those of the paper's table of isomorphism pairs: 3,359,232 for the first pair, which is 2 times 1,296 squared, so the graphs are isomorphic; 559,872 for the second, which is 1,296 times 432, so they are not. Each union has 1,728 vertices and takes under a minute. An actual isomorphism is any generator that maps a vertex of the first copy into the second.
